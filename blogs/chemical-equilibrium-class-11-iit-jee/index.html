Chapter Overview: Understanding Chemical Equilibrium

Chemical equilibrium is the state where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. At this point, concentrations of reactants and products remain constant over time.

This does not mean the reaction has stopped. Both forward and reverse reactions continue at equal speeds. This dynamic nature is the single most important idea to remember in this chapter.

The chapter builds directly on earlier concepts of reaction rates and reversible reactions. It also forms the base for ionic equilibrium, which appears immediately after this chapter in the NCERT textbook.

Types of Equilibrium and Key Terms

Equilibrium can occur in different physical states depending on the system involved. Recognizing the type quickly helps you choose the correct formula during numericals.

Type of EquilibriumExampleKey Feature
Physical EquilibriumLiquid and vapor waterNo new substance formed
Homogeneous Chemical EquilibriumN2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3All species in same phase
Heterogeneous Chemical EquilibriumCaCO3 ⇌ CaO + CO2Species in different phases


Static equilibrium does not apply to chemical systems since molecules keep reacting continuously. Every genuine chemical equilibrium is dynamic in nature.

Important Formulas and Expressions

The equilibrium constant Kc is defined as the ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations at equilibrium, each raised to their stoichiometric powers. This single expression governs nearly every numerical in this chapter, which is why examiners at every level of Chemical Equilibrium Class 11 & IIT-JEE repeat it often.

For a general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the expression is:

Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / [A]^a [B]^b

Kp is used when the reacting species are gaseous, and pressures are known instead of concentrations. The relation between Kp and Kc is:

Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn

Here Δn is the difference between moles of gaseous products and gaseous reactants. This formula appears frequently in JEE Main numericals.

The reaction quotient Q has the same form as Kc but applies at any stage of the reaction, not only at equilibrium. Comparing Q with Kc tells you the direction in which the reaction will proceed.

Degree of dissociation, represented by alpha, measures the fraction of a substance that dissociates before reaching equilibrium. It is essential for solving vapor density and dissociation-based problems.

Common Mistakes Students Make in This Chapter

Students frequently confuse Kc with Kp and apply the wrong expression during pressure-based problems. Always check whether the question provides concentrations or partial pressures before selecting a formula.

Another frequent error involves ignoring the phase of a substance in heterogeneous equilibrium. Pure solids and liquids should never appear in the equilibrium constant expression.

Many students also misapply Le Chatelier's principle by assuming that adding a catalyst shifts equilibrium. A catalyst only speeds up the attainment of equilibrium and does not change its position at all.

Sign errors while calculating Δn for the Kp and Kc relation are also common. A negative Δn value is easy to overlook under exam pressure, so double-check gaseous mole counts carefully.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the difference between Kc and Kp in chemical equilibrium?

Kc is calculated using molar concentrations, while Kp uses partial pressures of gaseous reactants and products. Both are related through the formula Kp equals Kc multiplied by RT raised to Δn.

2. Why are pure solids and liquids excluded from the equilibrium constant expression?

Their concentration remains constant regardless of the amount present in the system. Including them would not affect the numerical value, so they are omitted by convention.

3. How does temperature affect the equilibrium constant?

Temperature changes actually alter the value of the equilibrium constant itself, unlike pressure or concentration changes. For exothermic reactions, increasing temperature decreases the equilibrium constant value.

4. What is the best way to solve the degree of dissociation problems?

Set up an initial, change, and equilibrium table using variable alpha for the dissociated amount. Then substitute total moles into the vapor density formula to solve for alpha directly.

5. Does adding a catalyst change the position of chemical equilibrium?

No, a catalyst only reduces the time needed to reach equilibrium. It speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally without shifting the equilibrium position.

6. Which numericals are most important for JEE Main from this chapter?

Kp and Kc conversion problems, reaction quotient comparisons, and degree of dissociation questions appear most frequently. Practicing these three question types builds strong exam readiness for this chapter.

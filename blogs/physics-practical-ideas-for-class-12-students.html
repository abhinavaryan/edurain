Top 20 Physics Practical Ideas for Class 12 Students: Easy Experiments & Project Ideas | EduRain Blog

Good Class 12 Physics practical ideas combine a clear aim, simple apparatus, and a result students can explain in the viva. This guide lists twenty experiments and projects, from board-style circuit work to home-friendly models, so students can choose confidently among the best Physics Practical Ideas for Class 12 Students.

What do the CBSE Class 12 Physics practicals include?

CBSE Class 12 Physics practicals carry 30 marks. Students perform two experiments, one activity, maintain a record, submit an investigatory project, and face a viva. Always confirm the latest details in the official CBSE curriculum for your session.

ComponentMarks
Two experiments, one from each section7 + 7
Practical record (experiments and activities)5
One activity3
Investigatory project3
Viva on experiments, activities and project5
Total30

The record should contain at least 8 experiments, with 4 from each section, and 6 activities, with 3 from each section. It should also include the project report. Every CBSE Class 12 Physics practical file follows this basic pattern.

How to perform four key practicals step by step?

Each practical follows the same flow: connect the setup, take readings, calculate, and compare. The four procedures below cover circuit work, optics, semiconductors, and a home experiment that students can practise before the examination.

Practical 1: Resistance of a wire using a metre bridge

Apparatus

Metre bridge, cell, key, galvanometer, resistance box, unknown wire, jockey, connecting wires. Refer to Fig. 1 above.

  1. Connect the circuit as shown in the diagram and check all plugs and tight connections.
  2. Take a resistance R from the box and close the key.
  3. Touch the jockey near both ends. The galvanometer should deflect in opposite directions.
  4. Find the balance point l where the deflection is zero.
  5. Calculate X = R(100 − l)/l.
  6. Repeat for five values of R and take the mean of X.

To find resistivity, measure the wire length L and radius r with a screw gauge. Then use ρ = Xπr²/L.

Practical 2: Focal length of a convex lens by the u and v method

Apparatus

Optical bench, convex lens, lens holder, object needle, image screen, index needle. Refer to Fig. 2 above.

  1. Find the rough focal length by focusing a distant object on the screen.
  2. Fix the lens in the middle of the bench and place the object beyond the focal length.
  3. Move the screen until a sharp inverted image forms.
  4. Note the object distance u and image distance v.
  5. Repeat for at least six positions of the object.
  6. Use the Cartesian sign convention in 1/f = 1/v − 1/u and find the mean f. A graph of 1/v against 1/u also gives f.

Practical 3: Characteristics of a PN junction diode

Apparatus

PN junction diode, battery or variable supply, rheostat, voltmeter, milliammeter, microammeter, key.

Fig. 4. Forward bias circuit. The p-side of the diode joins the positive terminal through the milliammeter and rheostat. The voltmeter sits directly across the diode.

  1. For forward bias, connect the p-side to the positive terminal through the milliammeter and rheostat.
  2. Place the voltmeter across the diode.
  3. Increase the voltage in small steps and note the current each time.
  4. For reverse bias, reverse the diode and use the microammeter. Stay within the voltage limit in your manual.
  5. Plot both graphs. Mark the knee voltage and calculate the dynamic resistance from the slope.

Practical 4: Diffraction of light using a CD

Materials

Old CD, torch, black card with a narrow slit, white wall or screen. Refer to Fig. 3 above.

  1. Make a thin slit in the card and cover the torch with it in a dark room.
  2. Shine the beam on the shiny side of the CD at a small angle.
  3. Observe the coloured spectrum on the wall.
  4. With a teacher-supervised laser of known wavelength, measure the diffraction angle θ.
  5. Estimate the track spacing from d = nλ/sin θ.

Safety: Never point a laser at anyone's eyes, and never look into a reflected laser beam.

Which detailed projects can students build for the practical file?

Four projects work well for the file: an LDR light meter, a bridge rectifier, a Malus law setup, and an induction coil. Each needs simple materials, gives measurable readings, and supports confident viva answers.

Project A

Light meter using an LDR

Aim: To study how the resistance of a light-dependent resistor changes with the distance of a lamp.

Materials

LDR, digital multimeter, small bulb or torch, metre scale, a cardboard tube to block stray light, connecting wires.

Build and procedure

  1. Fix the LDR at one end of the metre scale and shield it from room light with the tube.
  2. Set the multimeter to resistance mode and connect it across the LDR.
  3. Place the lamp 10 cm away and note the resistance R.
  4. Move the lamp in steps of 10 cm up to 80 cm, noting R each time.
  5. Calculate 1/d² for every distance and plot R against 1/d².

Expected observation

Resistance falls as the lamp moves closer because the light intensity rises as 1/d² for a small source. The graph is a falling curve.

Viva questions

  1. Why does the resistance of an LDR decrease in light?
  2. Which material is commonly used in an LDR?

Project B

Bridge rectifier with a filter capacitor

Aim: To convert low-voltage AC into DC and observe the effect of a capacitor on the output.

Materials

Four 1N4007 diodes, a 470 µF capacitor, a 1 kΩ resistor, a breadboard, a multimeter, and a low-voltage AC source of about 6 V supplied by the laboratory. An oscilloscope helps if available.

Build and procedure

  1. Join the four diodes in a bridge and connect the AC source across two opposite corners.
  2. Connect the load resistor across the other two corners.
  3. Measure the AC input and the DC output with the multimeter.
  4. Observe the output waveform on an oscilloscope if one is available.
  5. Connect the capacitor across the load, matching its polarity, and compare the output again.

Expected observation

Without the capacitor, the output is pulsating DC at twice the input frequency, so 100 Hz for a 50 Hz supply. With the capacitor, the output becomes much smoother. The peak output is lower than the input peak by about two diode drops.

Viva questions

  1. Why does a full-wave rectifier double the output frequency?
  2. What happens if the capacitor is connected with reversed polarity?

Project C

Verifying Malus law with polarising sheets

Aim: To show that transmitted intensity varies as cos² θ when polarised light passes through an analyser.

Materials

Two polarising sheets, a steady lamp, a light sensor such as an LDR with a multimeter or a phone lux meter app, a protractor, a cardboard tube.

Build and procedure

  1. Place the lamp, the first sheet (polariser), the second sheet (analyser), and the sensor in a line inside the tube.
  2. Set the analyser at 0° and record the intensity I.
  3. Rotate the analyser in steps of 15° up to 90°, recording I at each angle.
  4. Calculate cos² θ for every angle.
  5. Plot I against cos² θ.

Expected observation

The graph is close to a straight line through the origin, which supports I = I0 cos² θ. Stray light and an unsteady lamp are the main sources of error.

Viva questions

  1. What fraction of unpolarised light passes through a single polariser?
  2. What does the intensity become when the analyser is at 90°?

Project D

Electromagnetic induction coil

Aim: To demonstrate induced emf and Lenz's law by moving a magnet through a coil.

Materials

About 200 turns of enamelled copper wire on a cardboard tube, a bar magnet, a centre-zero galvanometer or a multimeter on a small current range, connecting wires.

Build and procedure

  1. Scrape the wire ends and connect the coil to the galvanometer.
  2. Push the north pole of the magnet into the coil quickly and note the deflection.
  3. Pull the magnet out and note the direction of the deflection.
  4. Hold the magnet still inside the coil.
  5. Repeat the motion slowly, then use a coil with more turns.

Expected observation

The deflection reverses when the magnet moves out instead of in. Faster motion gives a larger deflection. A stationary magnet gives none. More turns give a larger deflection.

Viva questions

  1. State Lenz's law and link it to the direction of the deflection.
  2. Why does the speed of the magnet change the induced emf?

How can students prepare a Physics investigatory project for Class 12?

A Physics investigatory project needs a clear aim, a testable question, careful readings, and an honest conclusion. Students should pick one idea from the list above and document it in a fixed format.

  1. Choose one topic and write the aim in a single sentence.
  2. List the apparatus and draw a labelled diagram.
  3. Write the theory with the relevant formula and its meaning.
  4. Perform the activity and note at least five readings.
  5. Calculate the result, plot a graph, and mention the percentage error.
  6. Add precautions, sources of error, the conclusion, and a bibliography.

Students preparing a Physics investigatory project for Class 12 should be able to explain every line of their report. Examiners usually ask why a particular apparatus was chosen.

What common mistakes lower practical marks, and how can students avoid them?

Most lost marks come from missing units, untidy tables, copied observations, and weak viva answers. Students can avoid these errors by practising each experiment twice and writing every result in their own words.

Common mistakeBetter approach
Ignoring least count and zero errorNote both before taking any reading
Wrong sign convention in lens or mirror workApply the Cartesian sign convention every time
Graphs without labelled axesWrite the quantity, unit, and scale on each axis
Generic precautionsLink each precaution to the actual setup
Memorised viva answersUnderstand the principle behind each formula

Key Takeaways

  1. CBSE Class 12 Physics practicals carry 30 marks, so they deserve regular preparation.
  2. Section A covers electricity and magnetism, and Section B covers optics and electronics.
  3. Choose projects with measurable readings, a clear principle, and safe low-voltage material.
  4. Practise the full procedure of each practical, from circuit connection to final calculation.
  5. Be ready to explain the theory, formula, and precautions behind every experiment.

Conclusion and Next Steps

Strong practical marks come from understanding, not memorising. Select two experiments from each section and one project that interests you. Perform each one twice, then prepare short viva answers. Check your final list against the official CBSE curriculum and your school's laboratory manual.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

FAQ 1: What are the best Physics practical ideas for Class 12?

Metre bridge, potentiometer, convex lens focal length, PN junction diode characteristics, and galvanometer conversion are dependable choices. They have clear procedures, measurable readings, and frequent viva questions.

FAQ 2: Which Physics project is easiest for Class 12 students?

Projects such as light-dependent resistor response, diffraction using a CD, or a simple rectifier circuit are manageable. They need little material and demonstrate one principle clearly.

FAQ 3: Which Physics experiment can be done at home?

Diffraction with a CD, Malus law with polarising sheets, and an electromagnetic induction demonstration with a coil and magnet are possible at home. Use only cells or low-voltage sources.

FAQ 4: How to prepare a Physics practical file for Class 12?

Include the aim, apparatus, theory, procedure, observation tables, calculations, result, and precautions for each experiment. Add activities and the investigatory project report, and follow your school's prescribed format.

FAQ 5: How many experiments are required in Class 12 Physics practicals?

The record should contain at least 8 experiments, with 4 from each section, plus 6 activities, with 3 from each section. In the examination, students perform two experiments, one from each section.

FAQ 6: What materials are needed for Class 12 Physics projects?

Common materials include cells, connecting wires, a breadboard, LEDs, diodes, resistors, magnets, copper wire, a multimeter, and cardboard. The exact list depends on the chosen project.

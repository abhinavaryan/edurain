Nervous System and Reflex Actions Explained

How Does a Neuron Carry an Impulse?

A neuron is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. It carries electrical impulses from receptors to the brain, and then to muscles or glands. Its parts are the dendrite, cell body, axon, and nerve ending.

The impulse enters at the dendrite tip, moves through the cell body, and travels along the axon. At the nerve ending, a chemical crosses the gap called the synapse. It then starts a fresh impulse in the next neuron.

What Is a Reflex Action?

A reflex action is a sudden, automatic response to a stimulus without conscious thought. The spinal cord handles the response, so the brain does not control the action. Pulling your hand from fire is a common example.

The path of a reflex action is called the reflex arc. Follow these steps in order:

  1. The receptor in the skin detects heat.
  2. The sensory neuron carries the impulse to the spinal cord.
  3. The relay neuron connects the sensory neuron to the motor neuron.
  4. The motor neuron carries the impulse to the muscle.
  5. The effector muscle contracts and pulls the hand away.

Thinking takes time, so the reflex arc saves the body from harm quickly. The brain learns about the event only afterward.

Human Brain and Its Protection

The human brain has three main parts: forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain. Each part controls specific functions. The skull and cerebrospinal fluid protect the brain, and the vertebral column protects the spinal cord.

Brain PartMain Function
Forebrain (cerebrum)Thinking, memory, hearing, smell, sight, and hunger control
MidbrainLinks forebrain and hindbrain, and supports reflex responses
Hindbrain: cerebellumBody posture, balance, and precision of voluntary actions
Hindbrain: medullaInvoluntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation, and vomiting

Hormones in Animals and Plants Explained

Hormones are chemical messengers that control growth, metabolism, and behaviour. Animal glands release them into the blood. Plant cells produce them and send them to nearby tissues. Each hormone acts on specific target cells.

Animal Hormones and Their Functions

GlandHormoneFunction and Related Disorder
PituitaryGrowth hormoneControls growth; low level causes dwarfism
ThyroidThyroxineRegulates metabolism; iodine deficiency causes goitre
PancreasInsulinLowers blood sugar; low level causes diabetes
AdrenalAdrenalineRaises heartbeat for fight-or-flight responses
TestesTestosteroneBrings male changes at puberty
OvaryOestrogenBrings female changes at puberty


Feedback mechanism controls hormone quantity. When blood sugar rises, the pancreas releases more insulin. When sugar falls, insulin release drops. This keeps the level balanced.

Plant Movements and Plant Hormones

Plants respond to stimuli through directional growth called tropism. Phototropism responds to light, geotropism to gravity, hydrotropism to water, and chemotropism to chemicals. The touch-me-not leaf is different. It droops quickly because cells change their water content, with no growth involved.

Plant HormoneMain Function
AuxinMakes cells elongate; bends the shoot toward light
GibberellinsHelp stem growth
CytokininsPromote cell division, mostly in fruits and seeds
Abscisic acidInhibits growth and causes leaves to wilt

Common Mistakes in Control and Coordination

Most errors in this chapter come from mixing up similar terms. Watch for these mistakes:

  1. Mixing brain and spinal cord: The spinal cord handles reflex actions.
  2. Skipping the relay neuron: Many students break the reflex arc order.
  3. Swapping sensory and motor neurons: Sensory carries impulses to the centre. The motor carries them away.
  4. Wrong cerebellum function: It controls balance, not thinking.
  5. Confusing insulin and adrenaline: Insulin lowers sugar. Adrenaline prepares the body for emergencies.
  6. Linking thyroxine with calcium: It needs iodine.
  7. Calling touch me is not a growth movement: It is a quick response without growth.
  8. Incomplete diagrams: Missing labels and arrows lose marks.

Key Takeaways

  1. The reflex arc has five stages: receptor, sensory neuron, relay neuron, motor neuron, effector.
  2. The spinal cord controls reflex actions, while the cerebrum handles thinking.
  3. The cerebellum manages balance, and the medulla manages involuntary actions.
  4. Auxin, gibberellins, and cytokinins promote growth. Abscisic acid inhibits it.
  5. Insulin and thyroxine are high-priority hormones. Learn their disorders.
  6. Practice labelled diagrams daily, because they appear often in board papers.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the difference between a reflex action and a voluntary action?

A reflex action is automatic and controlled by the spinal cord. A voluntary action needs thought and is controlled by the forebrain.

2. What is the correct order of a reflex arc?

Receptor, sensory neuron, relay neuron, motor neuron, and effector. Write it as a flow chart in the exam.

3. Which hormones are most important for the board exam?

Focus on insulin, thyroxine, adrenaline, and growth hormone. Also learn auxin, gibberellins, cytokinins, and abscisic acid.

4. How do plant hormones cause phototropism?

Auxin moves to the shaded side of the shoot. Cells there elongate more, so the shoot bends toward light.

5. Why is iodised salt recommended for thyroxine?

The thyroid needs iodine to make thyroxine. Iodine deficiency causes goitre, which is a swelling of the neck.

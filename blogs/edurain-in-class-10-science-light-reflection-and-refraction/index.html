What Is Reflection of Light?

Reflection is the bouncing back of light when it strikes a polished or shiny surface, such as a mirror. This is the reason you can see your image in a mirror.

There are two laws of reflection:

  1. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
  2. The incident ray, reflected ray, and the normal all lie in the same plane.

Types of reflection:

  1. Regular reflection occurs on smooth, polished surfaces such as plane mirrors.
  2. Diffused reflection occurs on rough surfaces where light scatters in different directions.

Students should remember that reflection happens on all surfaces, but we can observe clear images only from smooth surfaces.

How Do Spherical Mirrors Work?


Spherical mirrors are mirrors with curved reflecting surfaces. There are two types:

  1. Concave mirrors – The reflecting surface curves inward. These are used in torches, headlights, and by dentists to see enlarged images of teeth.
  2. Convex mirrors – The reflecting surface curves outward. These are commonly used as rear view mirrors in vehicles because they provide a wider field of view.

Important Terms to Know

  1. Centre of curvature (C): The centre of the sphere from which the mirror is a part.
  2. Radius of curvature (R): The radius of that sphere.
  3. Principal axis: A straight line passing through the pole and centre of curvature.
  4. Focus (F): The point where parallel rays converge (concave) or appear to diverge from (convex).
  5. Focal length (f): The distance between the pole and the focus. The relationship is R = 2f.

What Is Refraction of Light?


Refraction is the bending of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another. For example, a pencil placed in a glass of water appears bent at the surface. This happens because light changes speed when moving between different media.

Laws of Refraction (Snell's Law):

  1. The incident ray, refracted ray, and normal all lie in the same plane.
  2. The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant for a given pair of media. This constant is called the refractive index.

Refractive index formula:

n = sin i/sin r

A higher refractive index means the medium is optically denser and light bends more when entering it.

How Do Lenses Refract Light?

A lens is a transparent material bounded by two curved surfaces. There are two types:

  1. Convex lens (converging lens): Thicker at the centre. It brings parallel rays of light to a focus.
  2. Concave lens (diverging lens): Thinner at the centre. It spreads parallel rays of light outward.

Image Formation by Convex Lens

  1. When the object is beyond 2F, the image is real, inverted, and smaller.
  2. When the object is at 2F, the image is real, inverted, and the same size.
  3. When the object is between F and 2F, the image is real, inverted, and enlarged.
  4. When the object is at F, the image forms at infinity.
  5. When the object is between F and the optical centre, the image is virtual, erect, and enlarged.

A concave lens always forms a virtual, erect, and smaller image.

What Are the Mirror and Lens Formulas?

These formulas are essential for solving numerical problems in Class 10 Science Reflection and Refraction.

Mirror formula:

1/v + 1/u = 1/f

Lens formula:

1/v – 1/u = 1/f

Magnification for mirrors:

m = –v/u

Magnification for lenses:

m = v/u

Sign Convention (New Cartesian)

  1. All distances are measured from the pole (mirrors) or optical centre (lenses).
  2. Distances in the direction of the incident light are positive.
  3. Distances opposite to the incident light are negative.
  4. Heights above the principal axis are positive. The heights below are negative.

Practical tip: Always draw a ray diagram before solving numericals. It helps you verify the sign of each value and avoid silly mistakes.

Common Mistakes Students Must Avoid

Many students lose marks in this chapter due to small errors. Here are the most frequent ones:

  1. Forgetting sign convention: This is the number one reason for wrong answers in numericals. Practice applying signs before substituting values.
  2. Confusing real and virtual images: Real images are formed on the same side as the reflected or refracted light. Virtual images are formed on the opposite side and cannot be caught on a screen.
  3. Mixing up concave and convex: Remember that concave means "caving in" and convex means "curving out."
  4. Skipping ray diagrams: Examiners often award separate marks for diagrams. Always draw them neatly with a ruler and label all points.
  5. Not practising enough numericals: This chapter requires regular practice. Solve at least 20 to 30 numerical problems from different sources.

Key Takeaways

  1. Reflection is the bouncing of light from a surface, and refraction is the bending of light between two media.
  2. Concave mirrors can form both real and virtual images, while convex mirrors always form virtual images.
  3. Convex lenses can form both real and virtual images, while concave lenses always form virtual images.
  4. The mirror formula (1/v + 1/u = 1/f) and lens formula (1/v – 1/u = 1/f) are essential for solving numericals.
  5. Always follow the New Cartesian sign convention to avoid errors.
  6. Regular practice of ray diagrams and numerical problems is the key to scoring full marks.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. Is the reflection and refraction chapter difficult for Class 10 students?

No, it is not difficult if you understand the basic laws and practise ray diagrams and numericals regularly. Most students find it scoring with proper preparation.

2. How many marks does this chapter carry in the CBSE board exam?

This chapter is part of the "Light" unit, which typically carries around 7 to 8 marks in the CBSE Class 10 Science paper. The exact weightage may vary each year.

3. What formulas should I memorize for this chapter?

You must memorize the mirror formula, lens formula, magnification formula, Snell's law, and the relationship between radius of curvature and focal length (R = 2f).

4. Are ray diagrams important for the exam?

Yes, ray diagrams carry dedicated marks. Always draw them neatly using a ruler and label the focus, centre of curvature, principal axis, and image clearly.

5. Can I prepare this chapter using free online resources?

Yes, many websites and apps offer free video lessons, solved examples, and practice tests for this chapter. Combining textbook study with digital resources gives the best results.

6. What career fields use the concepts of reflection and refraction?

These concepts are fundamental in fields such as optics, photography, ophthalmology, astronomy, and optical engineering. A strong foundation in physics opens doors to many science and technology careers.

Class 10 Electricity: Electric Power and Energy – Formulas, Concepts & Numericals | EduRain Blog

Electric power tells you how fast a device uses energy. Electric energy tells you how much it uses in total. Together, they explain why a 100 W bulb costs more to run than a 40 W bulb. They also decide how your monthly electricity bill is calculated.

This blog covers the core definitions, the three power formulas, and the commercial unit of energy. You will also learn the heating effect of current, solved numericals, a study plan, books, and common mistakes.

Chapter Overview: What Electric Power and Energy Cover in CBSE Class 10 Science

Electric power is the rate at which electrical energy is consumed or delivered in a circuit. Its SI unit is the watt. Energy is power multiplied by time.

This topic sits in Chapter 11, Electricity, of the CBSE Class 10 Science syllabus. It follows Ohm's law and resistors in series and parallel. Check the latest CBSE syllabus and NCERT edition before you begin. Questions on Class 10 Electricity: Electric Power and Energy appear every year as short answers and numericals. Expect two to four marks from this topic in most papers. Case-based questions also use power ratings from appliances.

Electric Power Explained: Definition, Unit, and the Three Formulas You Must Know

Electric power is the product of potential difference and current. One watt is the power used when one ampere flows under a potential difference of one volt.

Power can be written in three forms, depending on what is given. Use Ohm's law to move between them.

FormulaUse It When
P = VIVoltage and current are known
P = I²RCurrent and resistance are known
P = V²/RVoltage and resistance are known


The rated power on a bulb is its power at the rated voltage. A 60 W, 220 V bulb uses 60 W only when connected to 220 V. At a lower voltage, it uses less power and glows dimly.

Electrical Energy and the Commercial Unit: Joules, Kilowatt Hours, and Bill Units

Electrical energy is the total work done by a current over time. It equals power multiplied by time. The SI unit is the joule, while households use the kilowatt-hour.

The commercial unit of energy is the kilowatt-hour, written kWh. It is the energy used by a 1 kW device running for 1 hour. One unit on your bill equals one kWh.

Conversion1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ JJoule


Learn the conversion value by heart. It is asked directly almost every year.

Heating Effect of Electric Current: Joule's Law and Its Everyday Applications

The heating effect is the conversion of electrical energy into heat when current flows through a resistor. Joule's law gives the heat produced as H = I²Rt.

Heat produced depends on three factors. It increases with the square of the current, with resistance, and with time. Doubling the current makes the heat four times larger.

Everyday devices use this effect. Electric heaters, toasters, irons, and fuse wires depend on it. A fuse wire has a low melting point, so it melts when the current becomes too high. This protects the circuit.

Bulb filaments are made of tungsten. Tungsten has a high melting point, so it stays solid while glowing.

Solved Numericals on Electric Power, Energy Consumption, and Heat Produced

Numericals on this topic need one correct formula and careful units. Follow the same method each time: write the given values, choose the formula, convert units, then solve.

Numerical 1. A bulb is rated 60 W at 220 V. Find its current and resistance.

  1. Current: I = P/V = 60/220 ≈ 0.27 A
  2. Resistance: R = V²/P = (220 × 220)/60 ≈ 807 Ω

Numerical 2. A 1000 W heater runs for 2 hours daily for 30 days. The rate is ₹7 per unit. Find the bill.

  1. Energy per day: 1 kW × 2 h = 2 kWh
  2. Energy for 30 days: 2 × 30 = 60 kWh
  3. Cost: 60 × 7 = ₹420

Numerical 3. A 20 Ω resistor carries 2 A for 5 minutes. Find the heat produced.

  1. Time in seconds: 5 × 60 = 300 s
  2. Heat: H = I²Rt = 4 × 20 × 300 = 24000 J

Practice the circuit questions too. In a parallel circuit, every device gets the same voltage, so P = V²/R compares them easily. In a series circuit, the current is the same, so P = I²R is the better choice.

Common Mistakes Students Make in Power, Energy, and Heating Effect Numericals

Most marks are lost through unit errors, not weak concepts. These mistakes repeat every year.

  1. Skipping time conversion: Minutes and hours must become seconds when using joules.
  2. Mixing kW with W: Divide watts by 1000 before finding kWh.
  3. Using the wrong formula: Choose P = V²/R when voltage and resistance are given.
  4. Confusing rated and actual power: A bulb gives rated power only at rated voltage.
  5. Forgetting the square: Heat depends on I², not on I.
  6. Writing no units: Final answers without units lose a mark.

Keep a small error notebook. Write the question, your mistake, and the correct step.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the SI unit of electric power?

The SI unit of power is the watt. One watt equals one joule of energy used per second.

2. How do I convert watt-hours into kWh?

Divide the watt-hours by 1000. For example, 500 W used for 4 hours is 2000 Wh, which equals 2 kWh.

3. Which formula of power should I use in a numerical?

Pick the formula that matches the given values. Use VI with voltage and current, I²R with current and resistance, and V²/R with voltage and resistance.

4. Why does a fuse wire melt when current is high?

A fuse wire has a low melting point. High current produces more heat through Joule's law, so the wire melts and breaks the circuit.

5. Why do heating devices use high resistance wires?

Heat produced equals I²Rt, so higher resistance gives more heat for the same current. Nichrome is common because it has high resistivity and a high melting point.

6. What is the value of 1 kWh in joules?

One kWh equals 3.6 × 10⁶ joules. This comes from 1000 W multiplied by 3600 seconds.

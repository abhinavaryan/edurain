Thermodynamics for Class 11 & NEET 2027: Laws, Important Formulas, Heat and Thermodynamic Processes | EduRain Blog

Thermodynamics links heat, work, and internal energy through a few powerful laws. This guide on Thermodynamics for Class 11 & NEET 2027 explains the laws, key formulas, and processes in simple steps. It follows the NCERT Class 11 Physics concepts that NEET questions commonly test. Use it for concept clarity and quick revision.

Thermodynamics Fundamentals for Class 11 and NEET

Thermodynamics studies how heat energy changes into work and internal energy in a system. It deals with large scale properties such as pressure, volume, and temperature, and ignores the motion of individual molecules.

Learn these basic terms first:

  1. System: the part of the universe under study.
  2. Surroundings: everything outside the system.
  3. State variables: pressure, volume, temperature, and internal energy.
  4. State function: a quantity that depends only on the initial and final states, such as internal energy.
  5. Path function: a quantity that depends on the process, such as heat and work.

Always confirm the current syllabus on the official NTA and NMC pages before planning. Syllabus documents can change by examination year. Chapter wise weightage is not officially published, so treat any count as an estimate.

The Zeroth, First, and Second Laws Explained

The zeroth law defines temperature through thermal equilibrium. The first law states energy conservation as ΔU = Q − W. The second law gives the direction of heat flow and limits the efficiency of engines.

Zeroth law: If body A and body B are each in thermal equilibrium with body C, then A and B are in equilibrium with each other.

First law: Heat supplied to a system either raises its internal energy or does work. In the physics convention, Q is positive when heat enters the system. W is positive when the system does work.

Second law: Two statements are important for NEET.

  1. The Kelvin Planck statement says no engine can convert all absorbed heat into work in a cycle.
  2. The Clausius statement says heat cannot flow from a cold body to a hot body without external work.

Essential Thermodynamic Formulas for Quick Revision

Most numerical questions use a small set of formulas. Learn them with their conditions, because each formula applies to a specific process or gas type.

  1. Work done by a gas at constant pressure: W = PΔV
  2. Work in an isothermal process: W = nRT ln(V2/V1)
  3. Adiabatic condition: PVγ = constant, and TVγ−1 = constant
  4. Work in an adiabatic process: W = nR(T1 − T2)/(γ − 1)
  5. Mayer's relation: Cp − Cv = R
  6. Ratio of specific heats: γ = Cp/Cv
  7. Monatomic gas: Cv = 3R/2 and γ = 5/3
  8. Diatomic gas (rigid): Cv = 5R/2 and γ = 7/5
  9. Heat engine efficiency: η = W/Q1 = 1 − T2/T1 (Carnot engine)
  10. Refrigerator coefficient of performance: COP = T2/(T1 − T2)

Here T1 is the source temperature and T2 is the sink temperature, both in kelvin.

A thermodynamic process is a path along which a system moves from one state to another. Each process keeps one quantity fixed, which simplifies the first law and the work formula.

On a P V diagram, the area under the curve gives the work done. A cyclic process returns to its starting state, so ΔU is zero. The net work then equals the net heat absorbed.

Heat Engines, Refrigerators, and the Carnot Cycle

A heat engine absorbs heat from a hot source, converts part of it into work, and releases the rest to a cold sink. Its efficiency is the work output divided by the heat absorbed.

The Carnot cycle has four steps: isothermal expansion, adiabatic expansion, isothermal compression, and adiabatic compression. It is the most efficient cycle between two given temperatures. Its efficiency depends only on those temperatures.

A refrigerator runs the cycle in reverse. It takes heat from a cold space and releases heat to the surroundings using external work. Its performance is measured by the coefficient of performance, not by efficiency. No real engine can be 100 percent efficient, because a cold sink at absolute zero cannot be reached.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Most errors in this chapter come from sign conventions and unit handling, not from missing concepts. Checking signs and units before calculating prevents many lost marks.

  1. Mixing sign conventions: Physics uses ΔU = Q − W. Chemistry textbooks often use ΔU = q + w, where w is work done on the system. Stay consistent with the subject you are solving.
  2. Using Celsius in formulas: Always convert temperature to kelvin.
  3. Applying isothermal work to adiabatic cases: The two formulas are different. Check the process first.
  4. Forgetting that ΔU depends only on temperature change for an ideal gas: It is zero for any isothermal process.
  5. Misreading P V graphs: Adiabatic curves are steeper than isothermal curves at the same point.
  6. Ignoring degrees of freedom: Cv and γ change with the type of gas.

Key Takeaways

  1. Temperature is defined by the zeroth law, and energy conservation by the first law.
  2. Internal energy is a state function. Heat and work are path functions.
  3. Learn the work, heat, and ΔU relations for all four processes.
  4. Adiabatic processes have Q = 0, so ΔU = −W.
  5. Carnot efficiency is 1 − T2/T1, with temperatures in kelvin.
  6. Check the official NTA and NMC syllabus for your examination year.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

FAQ 1: What are the most important topics in thermodynamics for NEET?

The first law, the four thermodynamic processes, P V diagrams, and Carnot engine efficiency are the most important topics. Questions on specific heats and degrees of freedom also appear often.

FAQ 2: Which formulas should students remember for this chapter?

Remember ΔU = Q − W, W = PΔV, the isothermal and adiabatic work formulas, Cp − Cv = R, γ = Cp/Cv, and η = 1 − T2/T1. Note the condition for each formula.

FAQ 3: How can students practise numerical questions from this chapter?

Start with NCERT examples, then solve topic wise NEET previous year questions. Write the given data, identify the process, choose the formula, and check units before the final answer.

FAQ 4: Why is internal energy zero in an isothermal process for an ideal gas?

The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature. In an isothermal process the temperature does not change, so ΔU is zero and the heat absorbed equals the work done.

FAQ 5: What is the difference between isothermal and adiabatic processes?

In an isothermal process the temperature stays constant and heat is exchanged. In an adiabatic process no heat is exchanged, and the temperature changes as the gas does work.

FAQ 6: Why are adiabatic curves steeper than isothermal curves on a P V diagram?

In an adiabatic expansion the gas cools, so pressure falls faster than in an isothermal expansion. At the same point, the adiabatic slope is γ times the isothermal slope, and γ is always greater than 1.

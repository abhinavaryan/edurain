Class 10 Maths Chapter 4 Quadratic Equations

Class 10 Maths Chapter 4 Quadratic Equations teaches students to solve equations of the form ax² + bx + c = 0, where a is not equal to zero. This guide covers the NCERT methods, key formulas, solved examples, and important practice questions. Use it to revise the chapter clearly before the CBSE board examination.

What Does This Chapter Cover in the NCERT Syllabus?

The chapter covers the standard form of a quadratic equation, solving by factorisation, the quadratic formula, and the link between the discriminant and the nature of roots. It also includes word problems that lead to quadratic equations.

The CBSE curriculum asks students to find only real roots, using factorisation or the quadratic formula. Always confirm the current syllabus and marking scheme on the official CBSE and NCERT websites for your academic year.

Which Formulas Should Students Remember?

Students should remember the standard form, the quadratic formula, the discriminant, and the sum and product of roots. These four results solve most questions in this chapter, including the word problems.

The sum and product of roots are useful for checking answers. They are not a separate method in the CBSE syllabus.

How Do You Solve Quadratic Equations Step by Step?

There are two methods: factorisation and the quadratic formula. Use factorisation when the middle term splits easily. Use the formula when factors are hard to find or the roots are not whole numbers.

How Does Factorisation Work?

To factorise, split the middle term so that the two parts multiply to give a × c and add to give b. Then group the terms and set each factor equal to zero.

Example: Solve x² − 5x + 6 = 0.

Split −5x into −2x and −3x. This gives x(x − 2) − 3(x − 2) = 0, so (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0.

The roots are x = 2 and x = 3.

Example: Solve 6x² − x − 2 = 0.

Split −x into −4x and +3x. This gives 2x(3x − 2) + 1(3x − 2) = 0, so (3x − 2)(2x + 1) = 0.

The roots are x = 2/3 and x = −1/2.

How Does the Quadratic Formula Work?

Write the equation in standard form and identify a, b, and c. Calculate D, then substitute into x = (−b ± √D) / 2a. This method works whenever D is not negative.

Example: Solve 2x² − 7x + 3 = 0.

Here a = 2, b = −7, and c = 3. So D = 49 − 24 = 25.

Then x = (7 ± 5) / 4, which gives x = 3 and x = 1/2.

How Does the Discriminant Decide the Nature of Roots?

The discriminant D = b² − 4ac tells you the nature of the roots without solving the equation. A positive value gives two distinct real roots, zero gives equal real roots, and a negative value gives no real roots.

Value of DNature of Roots
D > 0Two distinct real roots
D = 0Two equal real roots
D < 0No real roots


Example: Find the nature of the roots of 3x² − 4√3x + 4 = 0.

Here D = 48 − 48 = 0. The roots are real and equal, and each root is x = 2√3/3.

Example: Find k so that kx(x − 2) + 6 = 0 has equal roots.

Rewrite the equation as kx² − 2kx + 6 = 0. Then D = 4k² − 24k = 0, so 4k(k − 6) = 0. Since k cannot be zero, k = 6.

What Are the Important Word Problems with Solutions?

Word problems ask students to form a quadratic equation from a situation and then solve it. Always reject answers that do not suit the context, such as negative lengths or negative counts.

Question 1: The product of two consecutive positive integers is 306. Find the integers.

Let the integers be x and x + 1. Then x² + x − 306 = 0, so D = 1 + 1224 = 1225 and √D = 35.

This gives x = (−1 + 35) / 2 = 17. The integers are 17 and 18.

Question 2: A rectangular plot has an area of 528 square metres. Its length is one metre more than twice its breadth. Find the dimensions.

Let the breadth be x metres. Then x(2x + 1) = 528, so 2x² + x − 528 = 0.

Here D = 1 + 4224 = 4225 and √D = 65, so x = (−1 + 65) / 4 = 16.

The breadth is 16 m, and the length is 33 m.

Practice: Solve x² − 7x + 12 = 0 and 5x² − 3x − 2 = 0. The answers are x = 3, 4 and x = 1, −2/5.

What Common Mistakes Should Students Avoid?

Most errors come from sign mistakes, skipped standard form, and unchecked answers. Writing each step neatly and substituting the roots back into the equation removes most of these errors.

  1. Not rearranging first: Move every term to one side so the equation equals zero before reading a, b, and c.
  2. Sign errors in D: When b is negative, b² is still positive. Write b² − 4ac with brackets.
  3. Dropping the ± sign: The formula gives two roots, so write both.
  4. Ignoring context: In word problems, reject negative values for length, age, or speed.
  5. Wrong middle term split: Check that the two parts multiply to a × c before grouping.

Key Takeaways

  1. A quadratic equation has the form ax² + bx + c = 0, where a is not equal to zero.
  2. Use factorisation for simple equations and the quadratic formula for all others.
  3. The discriminant D = b² − 4ac shows whether roots are distinct, equal, or not real.
  4. The sum of roots is −b/a and the product of roots is c/a.
  5. Always check word problem answers against the real situation.
  6. Write every step clearly, since board marking rewards correct method.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

FAQ 1: What are the most important topics in this chapter?

The key topics are the standard form, solving by factorisation, the quadratic formula, the discriminant and nature of roots, and word problems that lead to quadratic equations.

FAQ 2: Which formulas should students remember for this chapter?

Remember x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a, the discriminant D = b² − 4ac, the sum of roots −b/a, and the product of roots c/a.

FAQ 3: When should I use the quadratic formula instead of factorisation?

Use the formula when the middle term does not split easily or the roots are not whole numbers. Factorisation is faster when suitable factors are easy to see.

FAQ 4: What does it mean if the discriminant is negative?

A negative discriminant means the equation has no real roots. Since the syllabus asks only for real roots, you write that no real solution exists.

FAQ 5: How can students practise word problems from this chapter?

Define the unknown, form the equation from the given condition, solve it, and reject any answer that does not suit the context. Repeat this with varied NCERT questions.

FAQ 6: Is this chapter important for the CBSE board examination?

Quadratic equations belong to the Algebra unit, and questions on it appear in board papers. Check the current CBSE marking scheme for exact marks, since patterns can change.

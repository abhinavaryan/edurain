What Are Light Numericals and Ray Diagrams?

Light numericals are mathematical problems based on the laws of reflection and refraction, while ray diagrams are visual sketches that track the path of light to show how images form. Both topics form the core of the Class 10 physics syllabus under the CBSE and state board curriculums.

Understanding light requires students to connect theoretical laws with practical mathematical calculations. When you draw a diagram, you visually locate where an image forms. When you solve a numerical problem, you calculate the exact position, size, and nature of that image. Combining both methods gives you complete mastery over the chapter.

Key concepts in this unit include:

  1. Reflection of light by plane and curved mirrors
  2. Refraction of light through glass slabs and spherical lenses
  3. Image formation by concave and convex optical devices
  4. Real versus virtual images and their characteristics
  5. Refractive index, optical density, and lens power

The New Cartesian Sign Convention is fundamental for every calculation. Distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive, while distances measured against incident light are negative. Object distance (u u) is always taken as negative for standard setups. Concave mirrors and concave lenses have negative focal lengths, while convex mirrors and convex lenses have positive focal lengths.

How to Draw Accurate Ray Diagrams for Mirrors?

Drawing mirror diagrams requires following standard geometric rules where light rays reflect off curved surfaces according to the laws of reflection. You need at least two intersecting reflected rays to locate the image position.

Follow these fundamental rules when drawing mirror diagrams:

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through the principal focus after reflection from a concave mirror.
  2. A ray passing through the center of curvature reflects back along the same path.
  3. A ray directed toward the pole reflects at an equal angle with the principal axis.

Image Formation by Concave Mirrors

  1. An object at infinity produces a real, inverted, point sized image at the focus.
  2. Objects beyond the center of curvature form a real, inverted, diminished image between focus and center of curvature.
  3. An object at the center of curvature forms a real, inverted image of the same size at the center of curvature.
  4. The object between the center of curvature and focus produces a real, inverted, enlarged image beyond the center of curvature.
  5. An object at focus forms a real, inverted, highly enlarged image at infinity.
  6. The object between pole and focus forms a virtual, erect, and magnified image behind the mirror.

For convex mirrors, the image is always virtual, erect, diminished, and located behind the mirror between the pole and focus, regardless of object position.

How to Draw Ray Diagrams for Spherical Lenses?

Drawing lens diagrams involves tracing light rays that refract through transparent curved glass surfaces. Similar to mirrors, finding the intersection of two refracted rays gives you the image position.

Key rules for lens ray tracing:

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through the principal focus on the opposite side of a convex lens.
  2. A ray passing through the optical center emerges without any deviation.
  3. A ray passing through the primary focus emerges parallel to the principal axis after refraction.

Convex lenses are converging optical tools that can form both real and virtual images depending on where the object stands. Concave lenses are diverging tools that always form virtual, erect, and diminished images on the same side as the object. Always use a sharp pencil, a straight ruler, and label the optical center, focus points, and arrowheads clearly to earn full marks.

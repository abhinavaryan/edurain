Class 9 Science Chapter 9 Atomic Foundations of Matter

Atomic Foundations of Matter is Chapter 9 of the NCERT Class 9 Science textbook, Exploration. It follows Journey Inside the Atom and explains why atoms bond and how mass is conserved. Chapter Overview and Core Ideas

This chapter explains how atoms reach stability, combine to form substances, and keep total mass constant in chemical reactions. It also teaches how to calculate atomic, molecular, and formula unit masses.

Chapter 8 described the proton, neutron, and electron. Chapter 9 uses that knowledge to explain bonding and the laws of chemical combination. Dalton's atomic theory ties these ideas together. Check the current CBSE syllabus for your session before you begin.

Dalton's Theory and Laws of Chemical Combination

Dalton's atomic theory says matter is made of atoms that combine in fixed whole number ratios. It explains two laws: conservation of mass and constant proportions.

  1. Law of conservation of mass: Total mass of reactants equals total mass of products. Matter is only rearranged.
  2. Law of constant proportions: A pure compound always contains the same elements in the same mass ratio. Water has hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 1 : 8 by mass.
  3. Atoms of an element: All atoms of one element are alike in mass and properties, according to Dalton.
  4. Limitation: Atoms are divisible into subatomic particles, which you studied in Chapter 8.

Why Atoms Bond: Octet, Ionic and Covalent Bonds

Atoms bond to reach a stable outer shell. Most seek eight electrons, called an octet. Hydrogen and helium need two, called a duplet. Atoms transfer or share electrons to get there.

Sodium (2, 8, 1) loses one electron to become Na⁺ (2, 8). Chlorine (2, 8, 7) gains one electron to become Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8). The opposite charges attract and form NaCl.

Important Questions with Solutions

These questions follow the concepts of the chapter. Try each one first. Match your wording with the exercises in your NCERT textbook.

Question 1: 12 g of carbon burns in 32 g of oxygen. What mass of carbon dioxide forms?

By conservation of mass, the product mass is 12 + 32 = 44 g.

Question 2: How much hydrogen and oxygen are in 9 g of water?

The mass ratio is 1 : 8. So hydrogen is 1 g and oxygen is 8 g.

Question 3: Find the molecular mass of H₂SO₄.

(2 × 1) + 32 + (4 × 16) = 2 + 32 + 64 = 98 u.

Question 4: Find the molecular mass of CO₂.

12 + (2 × 16) = 44 u.

Question 5: Find the formula unit mass of CaCl₂.

40 + (2 × 35.5) = 40 + 71 = 111 u.

Question 6: Calculate the formula unit mass of NaCl.

23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u.

Question 7: Explain how sodium and chlorine form NaCl.

Sodium loses one electron and becomes Na⁺. Chlorine gains it and becomes Cl⁻. Their attraction forms an ionic bond.

Question 8: Why are noble gases chemically unreactive?

Their outer shells are already full. Helium has two electrons and the others have eight, so they need not gain, lose, or share electrons.

Question 9: Show covalent bonding in water.

Oxygen needs two more electrons. It shares one pair with each of two hydrogen atoms. Each hydrogen then has a duplet and oxygen has an octet.

Question 10: Write the formula of magnesium chloride.

Magnesium has valency 2 and chlorine has valency 1. Crossing them gives MgCl₂.

Question 11: Find the formula unit mass of Ca(OH)₂.

40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74 u.

Question 12: Which Dalton postulate explains the law of constant proportions?

Atoms combine in fixed whole number ratios. So a compound always has the same mass ratio of elements.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Most errors come from wrong charges, wrong atomic masses, and skipped steps. Each issue has a simple fix.

  1. Mixing bond types: Transfer means ionic. Sharing means covalent.
  2. Using wrong atomic mass: Use 35.5 for chlorine, not 35 or 36.
  3. Ignoring brackets: In Ca(OH)₂, multiply everything inside the bracket by 2.
  4. Calling NaCl a molecule: It is a formula unit of an ionic compound.
  5. Forgetting charges: Write Na⁺ and Cl⁻ with the correct signs.

Key Takeaways

  1. Atoms bond to achieve a stable outer shell.
  2. Ionic bonds transfer electrons and covalent bonds share them.
  3. Mass is conserved in every chemical reaction.
  4. A pure compound has a fixed mass ratio of elements.
  5. Add atomic masses carefully to find molecular and formula unit masses.
  6. Revise diagrams and numericals regularly.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

FAQ 1: What are the most important topics in this chapter?

Focus on Dalton's atomic theory, the two laws of chemical combination, the octet rule, ionic and covalent bonding, and mass calculations. These form the base of most questions.

FAQ 2: Which formulas should students remember for this chapter?

Remember that molecular mass is the sum of atomic masses in a molecule. Learn the same rule for formula unit mass, plus 1 u as one twelfth of a carbon 12 atom.

FAQ 3: How can students practise numerical questions from this chapter?

Write the atomic mass table first. Then solve molecular mass, formula unit mass, and conservation of mass questions in separate sets. Check each step and unit.

FAQ 4: What is the difference between molecular mass and formula unit mass?

Molecular mass is used for covalent substances made of molecules, such as H₂O. Formula unit mass is used for ionic compounds such as NaCl.

FAQ 5: Why do atoms form bonds?

Atoms bond to achieve a full outer shell, which is more stable. They gain, lose, or share electrons to reach an octet, or a duplet for hydrogen and helium.

FAQ 6: Is the syllabus for this chapter fixed every year?

Not always. Check the latest CBSE syllabus and the current NCERT textbook before preparing. Do not rely on old notes or unofficial lists.

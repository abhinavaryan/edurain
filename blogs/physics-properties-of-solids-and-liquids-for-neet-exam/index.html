Physics: Properties of Solids and Liquids Important Concepts, Formulas & Questions | EduRain Blog

This unit links elasticity, fluid pressure, flow and surface effects, and it mixes theory with short numericals. This guide explains the key concepts, formulas, solved questions and common errors. It suits students revising Physics Properties of Solids and Liquids from the NCERT Class 11 textbook for NEET.

Unit Overview for NEET Preparation

Properties of Solids and Liquids is a Class 11 Physics unit in the NEET syllabus. It covers elasticity, fluid pressure, Pascal's law, viscosity, Bernoulli's principle and surface tension, with NCERT as the main source for concepts and numericals.

Some syllabus listings also place heat and thermal topics near this unit. Confirm the exact topic list in the official NTA syllabus for your exam year. NTA does not publish chapter wise weightage, so treat any weightage claim as an unofficial trend.

Important Concepts and Subtopics to Master

Elasticity, Stress and Strain

Elasticity is the property by which a body regains its original dimensions after the deforming force is removed. Stress is force per unit area, strain is the relative change, and Hooke's law links them within the elastic limit.

There are three elastic moduli. Young's modulus relates to a change in length, bulk modulus to a change in volume, and the modulus of rigidity to a sliding deformation. Poisson's ratio compares lateral strain with longitudinal strain.

Fluid Pressure and Pascal's Law

Fluid pressure is force per unit area acting normally on a surface. In a liquid at rest, pressure increases with depth as ρgh, and Pascal's law says an applied pressure transmits equally throughout an enclosed fluid.

This idea explains the hydraulic lift and the hydraulic brake. A small force on a small piston creates a large force on a large piston, because pressure stays the same on both sides.

Fluid Flow, Bernoulli's Principle and Viscosity

Streamline flow, Bernoulli's principle and viscosity describe moving fluids. Bernoulli's principle relates pressure, speed and height for steady, incompressible, nonviscous flow, while viscosity explains internal resistance and the terminal velocity of falling bodies.

The equation of continuity says A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ for incompressible flow. A narrow section therefore carries faster flow. At very high Reynolds numbers, streamline flow turns turbulent.

Surface Tension and Capillarity

Surface tension is the force per unit length acting along a liquid surface, or equivalently the surface energy per unit area. It explains drops, bubbles, the angle of contact and capillary rise.

A liquid that wets glass has an angle of contact below 90 degrees and rises in a capillary tube. Mercury has an angle above 90 degrees and is depressed instead.

Solved Questions for Practice

Numerical questions from this unit mostly test substitution, unit conversion and the right choice of formula. Work through the three examples below, and take g = 9.8 m/s².

Example 1: A steel wire of length 2 m and cross section 1 mm² carries a load of 100 N. Find the extension. Take Y = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m².

ΔL = FL / (AY) = (100 × 2) / (1 × 10⁻⁶ × 2 × 10¹¹) = 1 × 10⁻³ m. The extension is 1 mm.

Example 2: Find the gauge pressure at a depth of 10 m in water.

P = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 10 = 9.8 × 10⁴ Pa.

Example 3: Find the capillary rise of water in a tube of radius 0.5 mm. Take S = 0.072 N/m and θ = 0.

h = 2S / (rρg) = (2 × 0.072) / (5 × 10⁻⁴ × 1000 × 9.8) = 0.144 / 4.9. The rise is about 0.029 m, or 2.9 cm.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Most lost marks come from unit errors and a wrong choice of formula. Convert every quantity to SI units before substituting, and check the physical meaning of the final answer.

  1. Using diameter instead of radius in Stokes' law and capillary rise.
  2. Forgetting that a soap bubble has two surfaces, so excess pressure is 4S/r.
  3. Using area in mm² without converting it to m².
  4. Mixing gauge pressure with absolute pressure.
  5. Applying Bernoulli's equation to viscous or unsteady flow.
  6. Writing terminal velocity with the body's density only, instead of ρ − σ.

Key Takeaways

  1. Elasticity questions depend on stress, strain and the right modulus for each type of deformation.
  2. Pressure in a liquid at rest is P₀ + ρgh, and Pascal's law explains hydraulic machines.
  3. Bernoulli's equation holds only for steady, incompressible, nonviscous flow.
  4. Terminal velocity depends on r², viscosity and the density difference.
  5. Soap bubbles have two surfaces, so use 4S/r for excess pressure.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

FAQ 1: What are the most important topics in Properties of Solids and Liquids for NEET?

Focus on Young's modulus, Hooke's law, Pascal's law, Bernoulli's principle, Stokes' law, terminal velocity, surface tension and capillary rise. These topics cover most concept and formula questions.

FAQ 2: Which formulas should students remember for this unit?

Remember Y = FL / (AΔL), P = P₀ + ρgh, Bernoulli's equation, Stokes' law, terminal velocity, excess pressure in drops and bubbles, and h = 2S cosθ / (rρg). The formula table lists them together.

FAQ 3: What is the difference between Young's modulus and bulk modulus?

Young's modulus measures resistance to a change in length under tension or compression. Bulk modulus measures resistance to a change in volume under uniform pressure. Solids have both, while liquids and gases have only bulk modulus.

FAQ 4: When can Bernoulli's equation be used?

Use it for steady, incompressible and nonviscous flow along a streamline. It does not apply to turbulent flow or to fluids with significant viscosity, so check these conditions before using it.

FAQ 5: Why is the excess pressure in a soap bubble 4S/r?

A soap bubble has two liquid surfaces, an inner one and an outer one. Each surface adds an excess pressure of 2S/r, so the total is 4S/r. A liquid drop has one surface, so its value is 2S/r.

FAQ 6: How can students practise numerical questions from this unit?

Solve NCERT exercises first, then move to previous year NEET questions on each topic. Convert all quantities to SI units, write the formula before substituting, and log every error for weekly review.

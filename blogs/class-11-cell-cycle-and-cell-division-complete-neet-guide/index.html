Class 11 Cell Cycle and Cell Division: Complete NEET Guide

This chapter in NCERT Class 11 Biology explains how cells grow, copy their DNA, and divide. Class 11 Cell Cycle and Cell Division is a high-yield unit for NEET UG Exams 2027. It tests both theory and diagram-based concepts. This guide covers the phases, mitosis, meiosis, a comparison table, a preparation plan, and common mistakes.

Cell Cycle Basics and Its Phases

The cell cycle is the ordered series of events in which a cell grows, duplicates its DNA, and divides into daughter cells. It has two parts: interphase, which includes G1, S, and G2, and the M phase, which includes mitosis and cytokinesis.

A human cell takes about 24 hours to complete one cycle. Interphase lasts more than 95 percent of this time. The M phase is the shortest part. Yeast completes its cycle in about 90 minutes.

PhaseMain eventsDNA content (2n cell)
G1Cell growth, metabolic activity, no DNA replication2C
SDNA replication, centriole duplication in animal cells2C to 4C
G2Protein synthesis, growth, preparation for mitosis4C
MKaryokinesis and cytokinesis4C to 2C


Some cells leave the cycle after G1 and enter G0, the quiescent stage. Cells in G0 stay metabolically active but do not divide unless signalled. During the S phase, DNA content doubles but the chromosome number stays the same.

Stages of Mitosis Explained

Mitosis is an equational division in which one parent cell forms two genetically identical daughter cells with the same chromosome number. It has four stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase.

  1. Prophase: Chromosomal material condenses. The centrosome moves to the poles and asters form. The nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear.
  2. Metaphase: Spindle fibres attach to kinetochores on the centromeres. Chromosomes align at the equator, forming the metaphase plate. This is the best stage for studying chromosome morphology and karyotyping.
  3. Anaphase: Centromeres split and sister chromatids move toward opposite poles.
  4. Telophase: Chromosomes decondense. The nuclear envelope, nucleolus, Golgi complex, and endoplasmic reticulum reappear.

Cytokinesis and Significance of Mitosis

Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm after nuclear division. Animal cells form a cleavage furrow that deepens and splits the cell. Plant cells build a cell plate that grows outward from the centre and becomes the new wall.

Sometimes karyokinesis occurs without cytokinesis, which produces a multinucleate syncytium. The liquid endosperm of coconut is a standard NCERT example.

Mitosis supports growth, repair of tissues, and replacement of worn-out cells. It also keeps the chromosome number constant across generations of somatic cells. In plants, it continues in meristems.

Meiosis I and Meiosis II Explained

Meiosis is a reductional division in which a diploid cell forms four haploid cells. It occurs in gamete mother cells, so it keeps the chromosome number constant across generations in sexually reproducing organisms.

Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes. Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, much like mitosis. A short interkinesis lies between them, with no DNA replication. Prophase I is long and has five substages.

SubstageKey event
LeptoteneChromosomes condense and become visible
ZygoteneSynapsis occurs and the synaptonemal complex forms, producing bivalents
PachyteneCrossing over takes place between non-sister chromatids, helped by recombinase
DiploteneThe synaptonemal complex dissolves, and chiasmata become visible
DiakinesisTerminalisation completes, the nucleolus disappears, and the nuclear envelope breaks down


In anaphase I, homologous chromosomes move to opposite poles while sister chromatids stay together. In some vertebrates, oocytes remain arrested in diplotene for months or years.

Mitosis and Meiosis Comparison Table

Mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same and produces identical cells, while meiosis halves it and produces variation. Examiners often frame questions as direct comparisons or as statement-based items.

Meiosis creates variation through crossing over and the random assortment of homologous chromosomes. These are major drivers of evolution in sexually reproducing species.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Most errors come from mixing up DNA content with chromosome number, or from confusing events between stages. Short comparison notes and labelled diagrams help students avoid these mistakes in the exam hall.

  1. Saying chromosome number doubles in the S phase. Only the DNA content doubles.
  2. Writing that sister chromatids separate in anaphase I. Homologous chromosomes separate in anaphase I. Sister chromatids separate in anaphase II.
  3. Mixing up crossing over and chiasmata. Crossing over happens in pachytene. Chiasmata become visible in diplotene.
  4. Forgetting plant and animal cytokinesis. Remember the cleavage furrow for animals and the cell plate for plants.
  5. Ignoring the G0 stage. Students often skip it, though it appears in NCERT-based questions.

Key Takeaways

  1. The cell cycle has interphase (G1, S, G2) and the M phase.
  2. DNA content doubles in the S phase, but the chromosome number stays the same.
  3. Mitosis gives two identical diploid cells, while meiosis gives four haploid cells.
  4. Prophase I has five substages, and crossing over occurs in pachytene.
  5. Anaphase I separates homologous chromosomes, and anaphase II separates sister chromatids.
  6. Labelled diagrams and comparison tables improve accuracy in NEET MCQs.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

FAQ 1: What are the most important topics in this chapter for NEET?

Focus on the phases of the cell cycle, the stages of mitosis, the five substages of prophase I, and the comparison of mitosis and meiosis. Questions on cytokinesis and the significance of each division also appear.

FAQ 2: Which numbers and terms should students remember?

Remember that a human cell cycle takes about 24 hours and interphase exceeds 95 percent of it. Also remember G0, 2C to 4C DNA content, bivalents, chiasmata, and the metaphase plate.

FAQ 3: In which stage does crossing over occur?

Crossing over occurs in pachytene of prophase I. It takes place between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes. The enzyme recombinase assists the process, and chiasmata become visible later in diplotene.

FAQ 4: Why is meiosis called a reductional division?

Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes, so the chromosome number falls from diploid to haploid. Meiosis II then separates sister chromatids, which is why it is called equational.

FAQ 5: How can students practise diagram-based questions from this chapter?

Draw each stage of mitosis and meiosis from memory, label chromosomes, centromeres and spindle fibres, then compare your sketch with the NCERT figures. Repeat this weekly until each stage becomes easy to recognise.

FAQ 6: Does DNA content or chromosome number double in the S phase?

Only DNA content doubles, from 2C to 4C. The chromosome number stays at 2n because the replicated copies remain attached as sister chromatids at the centromere.

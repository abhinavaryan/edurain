Light Reflection and Refraction Class 10: Notes & Tips

Light questions in the board exam follow a clear pattern. Most marks come from ray diagrams, sign convention and short numericals. These notes on Light Reflection and Refraction Class 10 cover the core concepts, formulas, diagrams, a study plan, recommended books and common mistakes.

Chapter Overview

This chapter explains how light bounces off polished surfaces and bends when it enters a new medium. It then applies these two ideas to mirrors and lenses. You need to know the laws, formulas and image positions.

The chapter has two parts. The first is reflection by spherical mirrors. The second is refraction through glass slabs and lenses.

The laws of reflection are simple. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane.

Refraction follows Snell's law. The ratio of sine of angle of incidence to sine of angle of refraction stays constant for a given pair of media.

Important Formulas

You need about ten formulas for this chapter. Each one links to a specific type of question. Learn the conditions under which each formula applies.

Formulas for Spherical Mirrors

The mirror formula connects object distance, image distance and focal length. Magnification tells you the size and orientation of the image. Both formulas need the Cartesian sign convention.

QuantityFormula
Focal length and radius of curvaturef = R / 2
Mirror formula1/v + 1/u = 1/f
Magnificationm = h' / h = − v / u

Formulas for Lenses and Refraction

The lens formula looks close to the mirror formula, but the sign between terms differs. Many students lose marks by mixing the two.

QuantityFormula
Refractive indexn = sin i / sin r
Refractive index and speed of lightn = c / v
Lens formula1/v − 1/u = 1/f
Magnification of lensm = v / u
Power of lensP = 1 / f (f in metres, P in dioptres)

Cartesian Sign Convention

The Cartesian sign convention fixes the signs of all distances. Measure distances from the pole or optical centre. Distances along the direction of incident light are positive. Distances against it are negative.

For a real object, u is always negative. A concave mirror has negative f. A convex mirror has positive f. A convex lens has positive f. A concave lens has negative f.

Ray Diagrams You Must Practice

Ray diagrams are the most reliable scoring area in this chapter. Draw at least two rays from the top of the object. The point where they meet, or appear to meet, locates the image.

For a concave mirror, three rays work well:

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through the focus after reflection.
  2. A ray through the focus becomes parallel to the axis.
  3. A ray through the centre of curvature retraces its path.

Image Formation by a Concave Mirror

The position of the object decides the nature of the image. Memorise this table and practise redrawing it from memory.

Image Formation by a Convex Lens

A convex lens follows the same logic. The object position again decides the image. Use two rays: one parallel to the axis and one through the optical centre.

A convex lens forms a real and inverted image when the object lies beyond F. It forms a virtual, erect and enlarged image when the object lies between F and the optical centre. This second case is the working principle of a magnifying glass.

Always mark arrowheads on rays and label F, C, P and O clearly. Examiners award marks for labels.

Common Mistakes in Light Reflection and Refraction

Most errors in this chapter come from signs, not from concepts. A small slip in sign changes the whole answer. Check each step before moving on.

  1. Wrong sign for u: Students write object distance as positive. It is negative for a real object.
  2. Mixing formulas: The mirror formula has a plus sign. The lens formula has a minus sign.
  3. Wrong focal length sign: A concave mirror has negative f. A convex mirror has positive f.
  4. Missing arrowheads: Rays without arrows lose marks in diagrams.
  5. Rays drawn as extended lines: Virtual images need dotted lines behind the mirror or lens.
  6. Forgetting units for power: Convert focal length to metres before finding power.
  7. Confusing real and virtual images: Real images form on a screen. Virtual images cannot.

Key Takeaways

  1. Learn the Cartesian sign convention first. Every numerical depends on it.
  2. Memorise the concave mirror image table and redraw it daily.
  3. Keep the mirror formula and lens formula separate in your notes.
  4. Convert focal length to metres before calculating power.
  5. Practise glass slab and lens diagrams with all labels.
  6. Solve NCERT fully before attempting any other book.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I solve mirror formula numericals easily?

Write the given values with correct signs first. Then put them into 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. Solve for the unknown and check the sign of the answer against the expected image type.

What is the best way to remember concave mirror image positions?

Use the table and redraw it from memory daily. Remember that images are real and inverted until the object crosses the focus. Between P and F, the image becomes virtual and erect.

Which book is best for numericals in this chapter?

Start with NCERT intext and exercise questions. Then use NCERT Exemplar for harder problems. Lakhmir Singh and Manjit Kaur adds more practice for objective and numerical questions.

What is the difference between the mirror formula and the lens formula?

The mirror formula is 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. The lens formula is 1/v − 1/u = 1/f. The sign between the terms is the key difference.

How do I find the power of a lens?

Power is the reciprocal of focal length in metres. Use P = 1/f. A convex lens has positive power. A concave lens has negative power. The unit is the dioptre.

How many ray diagrams should I practise?

Practise all six concave mirror cases, the convex mirror case, the glass slab and both lens types. Draw each at least three times before the exam.
